# Virtual hand tracking

This document describes the retained practice sandbox. For webcam control of the onion bench, see [README.md](README.md#webcam-hand-controls).

Run `npm run gesture:sandbox` and open http://localhost:3000. Click **Start camera** and allow webcam access. No package installation is required. Internet access is needed for MediaPipe, Three.js, and fonts. Use a current Chrome or Edge with WebGL enabled.

## Grab-and-cut sandbox

The page starts in **Mouse / keyboard** mode, so you can practice without webcam permission. With **Hand** selected, drag near the cube to grab it. Use the **Height** slider or Up/Down arrow keys while holding to lift or lower it, move it over the tray, and release. The cube settles under gravity, and placement completes when it lands inside the tray. With **Scalpel** selected, drag over the practice pad's dotted guide. The scalpel tool sets an initial height near the pad; adjust Height if contact is lost. Only strokes touching the pad count. Tracing at least 90% of the guide completes the cut task. Objects highlight within reach or on contact. **Reset practice** clears both tasks and restores the cube.

Cutting includes surface assistance. While the scalpel is selected or active, bringing the blade within 0.65 scene units of the pad locks its tip to the surface. The lock tolerates height jitter up to 0.95 units, then releases when lifted away or moved outside the pad. Grabbing is never snapped. A large crosshair/ring and a status pill above the workspace explain contact; no floating text covers the cutting indicator. Amber means locked to the surface; green means aligned with the guide. A translucent lane, larger dots, and a pulsing next-point marker show where to trace; cuts leave thicker marks. Lateral movement remains under user control. Hovering previews the lock without advancing progress; mouse dragging or the active scalpel gesture makes the cut. For mouse input, ray projection onto the pad keeps the visible reticle under the pointer despite imprecise height. Raw tracking position is retained so lifting away can unlock rather than becoming stuck to the pad.

Keyboard: **H** selects Hand, **S** selects Scalpel, **Up/Down** adjusts height, **+/−** and the mouse wheel zoom the view. Buttons and the height slider provide the same controls. Mouse input dispatches the existing six `input:*` events, with additive `source: 'mouse'` and exact scene coordinates in `world`. Pinch/cut events retain their normalized XYZ coordinates. The sandbox listens only to the selected input source.

Click **Use webcam pinch** or **Start camera** to use your virtual hand. Touch thumb and index together and move them toward the cube; pickup remains armed while the pinch is held, so it can latch when you enter reach. The grab point and 3D cursor are the midpoint of the virtual thumb/index tips. Hold the pinch to carry in XYZ and open the fingers to release. Pinch detection uses hand-size-normalized 3D distance with hysteresis (0.36 start / 0.52 end), falling back to aspect-corrected image distance. The occlusion guard does not reject touching fingers, and a second visible hand does not interrupt a pinch for zoom. Webcam pickup has slightly more reach tolerance than mouse input. Extend only the index to bring out the scalpel, then touch the blade to the dotted guide and trace it. The 3D cursor moves to the blade tip while the scalpel is active, so contact feedback matches the cutting point. Rendered scene coordinates are added to input events as `world`. Tracking loss, stopping the camera, switching sources, or cancelling mouse capture releases grabs and ends cuts. The pad is a practice surface; this does not implement specimen dissection.

`sandbox-state.js` implements reach checks, grab offsets, tray drops, contact-only cutting, guide coverage, teleport rejection, and reset independently of Three.js. `sandbox.js` renders the objects and consumes the input contract. `mouse-input.js` maps pointer movement and height to a 3D plane through the viewing camera.

Run `npm test` for unit/integration checks. Run `npm run test:sandbox` for the rendered mouse workflow in installed Google Chrome (override `PLAYWRIGHT_CHANNEL` to use another installed supported channel). Browser tests load the actual hand assets and Three.js locally, then grab, lift, place, cut, and reset through pointer/keyboard input. A screenshot is saved under ignored `test-results/`. Webcam recognition still requires hands-on testing.

## Camera lying flat (default)

Place the webcam on the desk with the lens facing **up**, and hover your hands above it, palms down and fingers reaching away from you. Select **Flat on desk, lens facing up**. Move left/right and forward/back over the lens to move across the virtual table. The flat-mode camera is steeper and the forward/back range is larger to make that travel visible. Camera depth toward the upward lens maps downward, so a down-facing palm and curling fingers face the table correctly.

Moving closer to the lens makes the entire hand larger and lower; lifting away makes it smaller and higher. Palm-only apparent size estimates distance relative to the first usable frame, with world-coordinate projection compensation when available. Finger curling is excluded from the size measurement. Size changes are smoothed and bounded from 0.6× to 1.6×. The complete model scales about the palm after fixed finger-length constraints, preventing fingers from staying the same length as the palm changes size. **Set neutral hand size** recalibrates at a comfortable working height. Calibration resets on camera setup changes, stopping, or a tracking gap longer than 1.5 seconds. Distance remains an approximation, especially with occlusion or large palm tilts.

Use **Camera rotation** (0/90/180/270 degrees) until moving right moves the virtual hand right and reaching forward moves it farther across the table. Both the webcam video and landmark overlay are mirrored horizontally. The flat-mode hand position and input cursor use that same horizontal direction, so physical left/right movement agrees across the preview and virtual stage. The reference remains at its original rotation. Pose rotation preserves handedness and depth. Changes release active gestures and reset smoothing.

With your palm facing down toward the upward-facing lens, extend only your index finger and curl the middle, ring, and pinky for 300 ms to bring out the scalpel. The thumb can rest naturally. The index can stay in the palm plane; it does not need to point away from the lens or toward a particular screen edge. Pinch and two-hand zoom remain available. These modes share the same six window event types.

The main workspace renders actual left/right skinned hand meshes from the WebXR Input Profiles generic-hand assets, stored locally under `assets/`. Each model has continuous skin, a shaped palm, webbing, and a 25-joint rig. All 21 MediaPipe landmarks drive the models; four missing metacarpal joints are reconstructed. Finger curling, spreading, and wrist orientation follow the camera input. Hand colors stay associated with stable handedness assignments. Tracking loss hides the corresponding virtual hand. The webcam reference and existing gesture events remain available.

## XYZ cursor and height

The cursor follows the rendered primary hand's index fingertip, including hand scaling and height. A bright marker, colored axis ticks, dashed vertical line, and table footprint make its 3D position visible. The info card displays normalized X/Y/Z and a height meter. Public **Z means vertical height**: 0 is the table, and 1 is 3.2 scene units above it; values are clamped to that range. Flat-mode distance-to-height gain spans the whole range within the allowed hand-size limits, rather than stopping around 60%. Set neutral hand size at a comfortable mid-height, then lower toward the lens for 0% or lift away for 100%. Finger articulation still affects the actual fingertip height. Three.js internally uses world Y for vertical. Height is estimated, not measured in centimeters. Internal `tracking:cursor3d` events carry the actual rendered fingertip position and normalized height; the next webcam input event uses that height sample.

The existing input contract gains an additive `z` field: `input:move` now sends `{ x, y, z, mode }`; pinch/cut start/end send `{ x, y, z }`. X/Y retain their existing normalized camera/table-plane mapping. Zoom remains `{ delta }`. Older callers that omit height receive neutral Z = 0.5. Resetting releases active actions at the last cursor height. Red cursor tick = X, green = Y along the table, blue = Z height.

Use **Angled view** to inspect the hand's depth. This changes the viewing camera without changing the tracking coordinates.

In **Facing you** mode, palm position maps directly into the virtual display using camera rays, covering 84% of its width and 80% of its height. Move your real hand left/right/up/down to move the virtual hand in those directions, with mirrored horizontal movement. The depth-based vertical control has been removed. A fixed interaction plane prevents palm-size noise from changing the model's apparent depth. Mapping adapts to display aspect ratio and the angled camera. The palm is wider at the wrist and thumb base, with a thicker rounded volume on both sides.

`PoseStabilizer` filters each hand once per webcam observation, with stronger smoothing for small changes and faster response for deliberate motion. Small deadbands suppress stationary jitter. Large position jumps and isolated world-joint outliers need a second consistent observation before they are followed. State resets after a tracking gap. The mesh has a short render interpolation rather than the earlier stack of strong filters. Palm translation and rotation always follow valid palm landmarks, even when several fingers are flagged as uncertain. Finger lengths come from the rig's actual proportions; observations and retargeted bones remain separate to avoid constraint feedback. Filtering adds some latency and cannot recover hidden joints.

**Movement sensitivity** defaults to 1.50×, amplifying webcam translation across the table and distance-driven height while preserving finger proportions. Adjust it from 0.75× to 2.50×; front-camera screen movement uses the same setting. **Gesture sensitivity** separately adjusts pinch thresholds and gesture zoom response. Mouse ray targeting remains direct for accurate picking and tracing.

`hand-pose.js` rebases wrist-relative world landmarks into a first-person palm frame. The left and right wrists enter from their respective sides, and fingers point forward and inward over the workbench. Palm roll is preserved relative to camera horizontal: turning your palm over turns the virtual hand over. The depth axis retains handedness instead of reflecting finger curls. Mirrored webcam movement controls bounded reach; apparent palm size estimates forward reach. This is a monocular approximation, not calibrated VR tracking or measured depth. Occluded fingers can be inaccurate. For best results, keep the whole hand in view with good lighting. Video stays in the browser.

`rigged-hand.js` retargets the WebXR mesh with palm-relative joint orientations and native bone proportions. Each bone receives an absolute position, orientation, and scale; the weighted skin deforms across its joints. The display no longer uses capsule fingers or a separately generated palm. Limited-visibility fingers briefly bridge 120 ms of tracking uncertainty, then follow the estimated pose with more smoothing instead of freezing indefinitely. Wrist proximity stabilizes hand identity if handedness labels flicker, and brief detection losses have a 180 ms grace period. This is not a collision solver; heavy occlusion can still produce intersections. Model-loading or WebGL failures display a message while leaving webcam gesture tracking available.

Run `npm install` once for the test dependency, then `npm test`. Tests load both real GLB files, retarget their bones, check finite skin vertices and bounds, and verify palm motion with occluded fingers, skin deformation during curling, distance scaling, gestures, and coordinate mappings. Starting the app still requires no npm installation. Live webcam pose accuracy and browser rendering need manual validation.

Manual check: start tracking, open and close each hand, pinch, rotate the wrist, show both hands, move closer/farther, toggle the angle, remove one hand, stop/restart the camera, and deny camera permission. The six existing `input:*` event types are unchanged; 3D poses are a separate visualization.

## Head-on fingers and occlusion assistance

Keep real fingers upright or diagonal to the camera; the first-person mapping still points the virtual fingers into the table workspace. An external webcam mounted above and to one side is another option for desk interaction. Avoid placing the camera directly in line with your fingers.

Occlusion assistance is enabled by default. `tracking-quality.js` checks projected segment lengths relative to palm size and estimated foreshortening. These are geometric heuristics, not model-provided visibility probabilities. Uncertain finger articulation is briefly held, then displayed with slower smoothing; uncertainty never freezes the palm. Pinch uses thumb/index distance rather than the curl/occlusion flags, since touching fingers naturally obscure each other. Scalpel requires a usable index pose; other uncertain fingers do not block it. Scalpel eligibility recovers after 120 ms of uninterrupted clear geometry. The UI lists uncertain fingers and explains how to adjust the view. Disable assistance to compare behavior.

## Index-only scalpel gesture

Extend only your index and curl the other three fingers for 300 ms to bring out a 3D scalpel. The classifier uses 3D world-landmark straightness, fingertip reach, and the other fingers' curl, independent of palm orientation or pointing direction. It falls back to aspect-corrected image landmarks when world coordinates are unavailable. Fold the index, open another finger, pinch, or leave the frame to put it away. Activation and release thresholds have hysteresis to reduce flicker. `input:cutstart` and `input:cutend` retain their existing contract. Two hands still take priority for zoom. The rendered tool follows the active virtual index finger; it does not yet cut a specimen.

This reduces jumps; it cannot recover unseen motion, guarantee absence of false positives, or resolve a confidently incorrect model estimate. A single RGB camera cannot directly observe joints hidden behind other joints. Test head-on pointing, rotating back to a clear view, curling a fist, hand crossings, and toggling assistance with a live camera. Synthetic tests cover foreshortening detection and recovery timing.
